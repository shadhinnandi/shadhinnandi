# Modular Arithmetic

## Why it matters

RSA, Diffie-Hellman, digital signatures and certificates all work with numbers **mod n**. Two skills come up in every paper:

1. Finding a **modular inverse**, such as d = e<sup>−1</sup> mod z in RSA.
2. Finding a **big power mod n**, such as 7<sup>927</sup> mod 13, without calculating the huge number.

## Basic rules

**a mod n** is the remainder when a is divided by n. Example: 27 mod 26 = 1.

You can reduce at any time, before or after multiplying:

```calc
(a × b) mod n = ((a mod n) × (b mod n)) mod n
a^{x+y} mod n = (a^{x} mod n) × (a^{y} mod n) mod n
a^{xy} mod n = (a^{x} mod n)^{y} mod n
```

A negative result is made positive by adding n: −5 mod 13 = 13 − 5 = 8.

## Modular inverse: what it means

**a<sup>−1</sup> mod n** is the number x such that (a × x) mod n = 1.

It exists only when a and n have no common factor (gcd(a, n) = 1). Example: 3<sup>−1</sup> mod 26 = 9, because 3 × 9 = 27 and 27 mod 26 = 1.

## Inverse by the normal method

Write the inverse as a fraction and add multiples of n to the top until it divides exactly.

```calc
a^{−1} mod n = 1/a mod n
             = (1 + n × k) / a mod n   [try k = 1, 2, 3, ... until a divides exactly]
```

**Example 1: 3<sup>−1</sup> mod 26**

```calc
3^{−1} mod 26 = 1/3 mod 26
              = (1 + 26 × k) / 3 mod 26
              = (1 + 26 × 1) / 3 mod 26   [k = 1]
              = 27 / 3 mod 26
              = 9 mod 26
              = 9
∴ 3^{−1} mod 26 = 9
```

**Example 2: 5<sup>−1</sup> mod 13**

```calc
5^{−1} mod 13 = 1/5 mod 13
              = (1 + 13 × k) / 5 mod 13
              = (1 + 13 × 3) / 5 mod 13   [k = 1 gives 14, k = 2 gives 27, k = 3 gives 40]
              = 40 / 5 mod 13
              = 8
∴ 5^{−1} mod 13 = 8
```

**Example 3: 7<sup>−1</sup> mod 160**

```calc
7^{−1} mod 160 = (1 + 160 × k) / 7 mod 160
               = (1 + 160 × 1) / 7 mod 160   [k = 1]
               = 161 / 7 mod 160
               = 23
∴ 7^{−1} mod 160 = 23
```

This method is fastest when k is small. When k gets large, use the Extended Euclidean method.

## Inverse by the Extended Euclidean method

To find a<sup>−1</sup> mod n:

1. Divide n by a. Note the quotient q<sub>1</sub> and remainder r<sub>1</sub>.
2. Divide a by r<sub>1</sub>, then r<sub>1</sub> by r<sub>2</sub>, and so on. Note each quotient q<sub>2</sub>, q<sub>3</sub>, ...
3. Stop at the division whose remainder is **1**.
4. Compute w values: w<sub>0</sub> = 1, w<sub>1</sub> = −q<sub>1</sub>w<sub>0</sub>, then w<sub>i</sub> = w<sub>i−2</sub> − q<sub>i</sub>w<sub>i−1</sub>.
5. The last w is the inverse. If it is negative, add n.

**Example 1: 3<sup>−1</sup> mod 26**

```calc
# Divisions
26 = 8 × 3 + 2   [q_1 = 8, remainder 2]
3 = 1 × 2 + 1    [q_2 = 1, remainder 1, stop]
# w values
w_0 = 1
w_1 = −q_1 × w_0 = −8 × 1 = −8
w_2 = w_0 − q_2 × w_1 = 1 − 1 × (−8) = 9
∴ 3^{−1} mod 26 = 9
```

**Example 2: 5<sup>−1</sup> mod 13**

```calc
# Divisions
13 = 2 × 5 + 3   [q_1 = 2]
5 = 1 × 3 + 2    [q_2 = 1]
3 = 1 × 2 + 1    [q_3 = 1, remainder 1, stop]
# w values
w_0 = 1
w_1 = −q_1 × w_0 = −2 × 1 = −2
w_2 = w_0 − q_2 × w_1 = 1 − 1 × (−2) = 3
w_3 = w_1 − q_3 × w_2 = −2 − 1 × 3 = −5
# Make it positive
−5 mod 13 = (13 − 5) mod 13
          = 8
∴ 5^{−1} mod 13 = 8
```

**Example 3: 7<sup>−1</sup> mod 160**

```calc
# Divisions
160 = 22 × 7 + 6   [q_1 = 22]
7 = 1 × 6 + 1      [q_2 = 1, remainder 1, stop]
# w values
w_0 = 1
w_1 = −q_1 × w_0 = −22 × 1 = −22
w_2 = w_0 − q_2 × w_1 = 1 − 1 × (−22) = 23
∴ 7^{−1} mod 160 = 23
```

**Check your answer:** multiply. 7 × 23 = 161, and 161 mod 160 = 1. Correct.

## Big powers: break the exponent

Never calculate the full power. Split the exponent so that a small power gives a small remainder (best of all, 1), then reduce.

**Example: 3<sup>10</sup> mod 13**

```calc
3^{10} mod 13 = (3^{3})^{3} × 3 mod 13
              = (27 mod 13)^{3} × 3 mod 13
              = 1^{3} × 3 mod 13   [27 mod 13 = 1]
              = 3
∴ 3^{10} mod 13 = 3
```

**Example: 7<sup>100</sup> mod 13 by repeated reduction**

```calc
7^{100} mod 13 = (7^{2} mod 13)^{50}
               = 10^{50} mod 13   [49 mod 13 = 10]
               = (10^{2} mod 13)^{25}
               = 9^{25} mod 13   [100 mod 13 = 9]
               = (9^{2} mod 13)^{12} × 9 mod 13
               = 3^{12} mod 13 × 9 mod 13   [81 mod 13 = 3]
               = 1 × 9 mod 13   [3^{12} mod 13 = 1 by Fermat]
               = 9
∴ 7^{100} mod 13 = 9
```

## Fermat's Little Theorem (the shortcut)

If **p is prime** and **a is not divisible by p**, then:

```calc
a^{p−1} mod p = 1
```

So when the modulus is prime, divide the exponent by (p − 1) and keep only the remainder.

**Example: 7<sup>100</sup> mod 13 by Fermat**

```calc
# 13 is prime, so 7^{12} mod 13 = 1. Divide 100 by 12: 100 = 12 × 8 + 4
7^{100} mod 13 = (7^{12})^{8} × 7^{4} mod 13
               = 1^{8} × 7^{4} mod 13   [a^{p−1} mod p = 1]
               = 2401 mod 13
               = 9
∴ 7^{100} mod 13 = 9
```

Both methods give the same answer. Fermat is faster in the exam when the modulus is prime.

## Big powers: square and multiply

When the modulus is **not prime** (as in RSA, where n = p × q), use repeated squaring:

1. Write the exponent as a sum of powers of 2.
2. Make a table: a<sup>1</sup>, a<sup>2</sup>, a<sup>4</sup>, a<sup>8</sup>, ... each one is the square of the one before, mod n.
3. Multiply the needed rows together, reducing mod n after each multiplication.

**Example: 3<sup>45</sup> mod 35**

```calc
# 45 = 32 + 8 + 4 + 1
3^{1} mod 35 = 3
3^{2} mod 35 = 9
3^{4} mod 35 = 9^{2} mod 35 = 81 mod 35 = 11
3^{8} mod 35 = 11^{2} mod 35 = 121 mod 35 = 16
3^{16} mod 35 = 16^{2} mod 35 = 256 mod 35 = 11
3^{32} mod 35 = 11^{2} mod 35 = 121 mod 35 = 16
# Multiply the rows for 32, 8, 4 and 1
3^{45} mod 35 = 3^{32} × 3^{8} × 3^{4} × 3^{1} mod 35
              = 16 × 16 × 11 × 3 mod 35
              = 256 mod 35 × 11 × 3 mod 35
              = 11 × 11 × 3 mod 35
              = 121 mod 35 × 3 mod 35
              = 16 × 3 mod 35
              = 48 mod 35
              = 13
∴ 3^{45} mod 35 = 13
```

## Exam-ready answers

**Q. Find 5<sup>−1</sup> mod 13. (2 marks)**

Use (1 + 13k)/5 and try k = 1, 2, 3. For k = 3: 40/5 = 8. Check: 5 × 8 = 40 = 3 × 13 + 1. So **5<sup>−1</sup> mod 13 = 8**.

**Q. State Fermat's Little Theorem and use it for 7<sup>100</sup> mod 13. (3 marks)**

If p is prime and p does not divide a, then a<sup>p−1</sup> mod p = 1. Here p = 13, so 7<sup>12</sup> mod 13 = 1. Since 100 = 12 × 8 + 4, 7<sup>100</sup> mod 13 = 1<sup>8</sup> × 7<sup>4</sup> mod 13 = 2401 mod 13 = **9**.
