# Argumentative Essay

## Definition and purpose

An argumentative essay **takes a clear position on a debatable question and tries to convince the reader** with reasons and evidence. It also considers the opposing view and answers it. Unlike a descriptive or cause-effect essay, it says what **should** be believed or done.

Argumentative topics are usually **questions with two possible answers**: *Should attendance be mandatory?* *Should…be banned?* You must choose one side and defend it throughout.

The slides model the same question from **both sides**: slide 3.1 argues that attendance *should* be mandatory, and slide 3.2 argues that it *should not*. This shows an important truth: marks come from **how well you argue**, not from which side you choose.

## Structure and outline template

The slides' argumentative essay has **five parts** and adds one item to the plan of development: the **refutation**.

| Paragraph | Purpose | In slide 3.1 |
|---|---|---|
| **Introduction** | Background on the issue, then a thesis that states your position and three reasons | *I strongly believe that attendance should be mandatory for university students because regular attendance improves learning, develops responsibility, and encourages active participation in class.* |
| **Body 1** | Reason 1, explained with examples | *Firstly, regular attendance helps university students understand their lessons better…* |
| **Body 2** | Reason 2 | *Secondly, mandatory attendance helps students develop responsibility…* |
| **Body 3** | Reason 3 | *Finally, attending classes regularly encourages students to participate…* |
| **Refutation** | State the opposing argument fairly, then answer it | *Some people argue that university students are mature enough to decide… However, a reasonable attendance policy can give students flexibility…* |
| **Conclusion** | Restate position and reasons; final recommendation | *In conclusion, attendance should be mandatory… A reasonable attendance policy can therefore help students…* |

### The slides' outline

```text
Topic:
Thesis Statement: I strongly believe that … because (reason 1), (reason 2), and (reason 3).
Supporting Points/Plan of Development:
  i) Reason 1
  ii) Reason 2
  iii) Reason 3
  iv) Refutation of the opposing argument
Topic Sentence of the First Supporting Paragraph: Firstly, …
Topic Sentence of the Second Supporting Paragraph: Secondly, …
Topic Sentence of the Third Supporting Paragraph: Finally, …
Refutation/Counterargument: Some people argue that … However, …
Concluding Sentence: In conclusion, it can be argued that …
```

### Key phrases from the slides

| Purpose | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Stating your position | *I strongly believe that…*, *I strongly support that…* |
| Ordering reasons | *Firstly… Secondly… Finally…* |
| Introducing the opposing view | *Some people argue that…* |
| Answering it (refutation) | *However,…* |
| Concluding | *In conclusion, it can be argued/claimed that…* |

## Counterarguments and refutation

A **counterargument** is the strongest point the other side would make. A **refutation** is your answer to it. Including both shows the examiner that you have thought about the issue fairly, which makes your argument stronger, not weaker.

A good refutation:

1. **States the opposing view fairly** in one sentence (*Some people argue that…*).
2. **Answers it** with *However,…*: show that it is wrong, exaggerated, or can be solved.
3. **Returns to your position.**

In slide 3.1, the opposing view (*students are mature enough to decide*) is answered with a compromise (*a reasonable policy can give flexibility while keeping students engaged*). In slide 3.2, the opposing view (*attendance improves performance*) is answered by pointing out that *motivated students can stay engaged without compulsory attendance*.

## Writing procedure

1. **Read the question** and decide your side quickly. Choose the side with **better reasons**, not your personal feeling.
2. **List three reasons** for your side and the **strongest reason** for the other side.
3. **Write the thesis** with the slides' pattern: *I strongly believe that [position] because [reason 1], [reason 2], and [reason 3].*
4. **Complete the outline**, including the refutation.
5. **Write the introduction**: background on the debate (both sides exist), then your thesis.
6. **Write three body paragraphs**: topic sentence (*Firstly,…*), explanation, example, result.
7. **Write the refutation paragraph**: *Some people argue that… However,…*
8. **Write the conclusion**: restate your position and reasons, end with a recommendation.

## Model essays

Two complete models with **different positions and approaches**: the first supports a ban, the second opposes abolishing something. Both follow the slides' five-part structure with a refutation.

:::model Model essay 1 · Supporting a position | Should Smoking Be Banned in All Public Places?
--- outline
- **Topic:** Should Smoking Be Banned in All Public Places?
- **Thesis Statement:** I strongly believe that smoking should be banned in all public places because it harms non-smokers' health, sets a bad example for young people and pollutes the shared environment.
- **Supporting Points/Plan of Development:** i) Harms non-smokers' health ii) Sets a bad example for young people iii) Pollutes the shared environment iv) Refutation of the opposing argument
- **Topic Sentence of the First Supporting Paragraph:** Firstly, smoking in public places seriously harms the health of non-smokers.
- **Topic Sentence of the Second Supporting Paragraph:** Secondly, smoking in public sets a harmful example for children and young people.
- **Topic Sentence of the Third Supporting Paragraph:** Finally, smoking pollutes public spaces that belong to everyone.
- **Refutation/Counterargument:** Some people argue that a ban limits smokers' personal freedom. However, personal freedom ends where it begins to harm other people.
- **Concluding Sentence:** In conclusion, it can be argued that smoking should be banned in all public places to protect public health and the shared environment.
--- answer Essay
Walking through a crowded bus stop, many people are forced to breathe in cigarette smoke without any choice. Smoking is a personal habit, but its effects often reach people who have never touched a cigarette. Bangladesh already has laws that limit smoking in some public places, yet smoking continues openly at tea stalls, bus terminals and markets. While some people defend smoking as a matter of personal choice, the harm it causes to others cannot be ignored. I strongly believe that smoking should be banned in all public places because it harms non-smokers' health, sets a bad example for young people and pollutes the shared environment.

Firstly, smoking in public places seriously harms the health of non-smokers. When a person smokes, the people around them inhale second-hand smoke, which contains many of the same harmful chemicals as the cigarette itself. Doctors warn that second-hand smoke increases the risk of heart disease, lung cancer and breathing problems. Children, pregnant women and elderly people are especially vulnerable. For example, a child waiting with a parent at a bus stand may breathe smoke from several smokers in a few minutes. Since these people do not choose to smoke, it is unfair that they should suffer its consequences.

Secondly, smoking in public sets a harmful example for children and young people. Young people learn by observing adults. When they constantly see people smoking openly in markets, parks and outside schools, smoking begins to look normal and even attractive. As a result, many teenagers start smoking out of curiosity or to look mature, and they often become addicted for life. A complete ban in public places would make smoking less visible and send a clear message that it is unhealthy and socially unacceptable.

Finally, smoking pollutes public spaces that belong to everyone. Cigarette butts are among the most common forms of litter on streets, beaches and parks, and they contain toxic chemicals that pollute soil and water. Smoke also leaves an unpleasant smell in waiting rooms, restaurants and public transport. Moreover, carelessly thrown cigarettes can start fires in crowded markets. Public places should be clean and safe for all, and smoking makes them neither.

Some people argue that a ban limits smokers' personal freedom. They believe adults should be free to decide what they do with their own bodies. However, personal freedom ends where it begins to harm other people. A ban in public places does not stop anyone from smoking privately; it simply protects others from being forced to share the risk. In the same way, people are free to play loud music at home but not in a hospital.

In conclusion, smoking in public places endangers non-smokers, influences young people negatively and pollutes spaces that everyone shares. A complete ban, properly enforced, would protect public health without taking away private choice. Therefore, it can be argued that smoking should be banned in all public places to protect public health and the shared environment.
--- note
The thesis uses the slides' phrase **I strongly believe that… because…** and lists three reasons in **parallel** form (*harms…, sets…, pollutes…*). The **refutation** paragraph states the opposing view fairly (*personal freedom*) and answers it with *However* and an analogy. The body paragraphs begin with **Firstly, Secondly, Finally**, as in the slides.
:::

:::model Model essay 2 · Opposing a proposal | Should Homework Be Abolished in Primary Schools?
--- outline
- **Topic:** Should Homework Be Abolished in Primary Schools?
- **Thesis Statement:** I strongly believe that homework should not be abolished in primary schools because it reinforces classroom learning, builds discipline and helps parents follow their children's progress.
- **Supporting Points/Plan of Development:** i) Reinforces classroom learning ii) Builds discipline and responsibility iii) Helps parents follow progress iv) Refutation of the opposing argument
- **Topic Sentence of the First Supporting Paragraph:** Firstly, homework helps young children reinforce what they learn in class.
- **Topic Sentence of the Second Supporting Paragraph:** Secondly, regular homework builds discipline and a sense of responsibility.
- **Topic Sentence of the Third Supporting Paragraph:** Finally, homework allows parents to follow and support their children's learning.
- **Refutation/Counterargument:** Some people argue that homework puts too much pressure on young children. However, the solution is to give short, suitable tasks, not to remove homework completely.
- **Concluding Sentence:** In conclusion, it can be argued that homework should not be abolished in primary schools but should be kept short, meaningful and appropriate for children's age.
--- answer Essay
Every evening, millions of primary school children sit at home with their notebooks, sometimes eagerly and sometimes with tears. In recent years, many parents and educators have questioned whether young children should be given homework at all. Some countries have reduced homework in early grades, and the debate has reached Bangladesh as well. Although too much homework can certainly be harmful, removing it entirely would take away a useful learning tool. I strongly believe that homework should not be abolished in primary schools because it reinforces classroom learning, builds discipline and helps parents follow their children's progress.

Firstly, homework helps young children reinforce what they learn in class. Children often understand a new idea during a lesson but forget it quickly if they do not practise it. A short task at home, such as writing ten new words or solving five addition problems, gives them a chance to use what they have learned while it is still fresh. For example, a child who practises reading aloud for fifteen minutes every evening usually reads more fluently than one who reads only at school. In this way, homework turns short-term understanding into lasting skill.

Secondly, regular homework builds discipline and a sense of responsibility. When children have a small task to complete every day, they learn to manage their time, follow instructions and finish what they start. These habits do not appear suddenly in adulthood; they are formed in childhood. A child who learns to sit down and complete a task before playing is preparing for the greater responsibilities of secondary school, university and work.

Finally, homework allows parents to follow and support their children's learning. Most parents cannot attend classes, but homework shows them what their children are studying and where they are struggling. If a child repeatedly finds spelling difficult, parents can give extra help or talk to the teacher early. Homework therefore creates a useful link between home and school and involves families in children's education.

Some people argue that homework puts too much pressure on young children and takes away their time to play. This concern is understandable, as heavy bags and hours of assignments can cause stress and tiredness. However, the solution is to give short, suitable tasks, not to remove homework completely. If schools limit homework to fifteen or twenty minutes and make it enjoyable, such as reading a story or observing plants, children gain its benefits while still having plenty of time to rest and play.

In conclusion, homework helps primary school children remember their lessons, develop good habits and stay connected with their parents' support. The problem is not homework itself but too much of it. Therefore, it can be argued that homework should not be abolished in primary schools but should be kept short, meaningful and appropriate for children's age.
--- note
This model **opposes** the proposal in the question, like slide 3.2 opposes mandatory attendance. Its **refutation** first **accepts part** of the opposing view (*This concern is understandable*) and then offers a **solution** (short tasks), a mature technique that also appears in slide 3.1. The conclusion turns the argument into a practical recommendation.
:::

## Examination strategy

- **Interpret the prompt.** A *Should…?* question needs a clear yes or no. Do not sit on the fence ("both sides have good points") in the thesis.
- **Organise quickly.** Draw two columns: *For* and *Against*. Choose the side with three strong reasons, and take the strongest point from the other column for your refutation.
- **Choose supporting points.** Reasons must be **different** (health, social, environmental; or learning, discipline, family). Avoid three versions of the same reason.
- **Avoid common errors:** forgetting the refutation paragraph, changing sides halfway, using emotional statements without reasons (*smoking is disgusting*), and attacking people instead of arguments.
- **Time:** outline 5 minutes (include the refutation line), essay 30 to 35 minutes, check 3 minutes.
- **Unfamiliar topic?** Reasons can almost always be found under **individual / social / economic** or **health / education / environment** headings.

:::practice Practice 2 · Refutation | Write the counterargument paragraph
Your thesis: *Online exams should replace paper exams at universities.* Write a refutation paragraph (80 to 110 words) using *Some people argue that… However,…*
--- skill
Stating and answering the opposing view
--- answer
Some people argue that online exams make cheating easier because students can search the internet or contact friends during the test. This is a genuine concern, especially when exams are poorly designed. However, universities can reduce cheating by using question banks that give each student different questions, setting strict time limits and asking analytical questions that cannot be answered simply by searching. Many online platforms also lock the browser during the test. With careful planning, online exams can be as fair as paper exams while offering faster marking and greater convenience.
:::
