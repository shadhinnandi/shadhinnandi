# Paragraph Structure

## What is a paragraph?

The slides define it simply: *a paragraph is a group of sentences about one particular topic or idea. It provides specific information on the topic. A good paragraph will not include irrelevant sentences. It will focus on the main idea.*

Every paragraph in good academic writing has **three parts**:

| Part | Position | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Topic sentence** | Beginning (introduction) | States the topic and the controlling idea |
| **Supporting sentences** | Middle (body) | Explain, prove and illustrate the controlling idea |
| **Concluding sentence** | End (conclusion) | Restates the main idea or sums up the paragraph |

The slides compare a paragraph to a **sandwich**: the topic sentence and the concluding sentence are the bread on the top and bottom, and the supporting sentences are the filling in the middle.

## Part 1: the topic sentence

The topic sentence is usually the first sentence. It tells the reader the main idea and contains two parts:

- **Topic:** what the paragraph is about.
- **Controlling idea:** what you will say about the topic. It limits the paragraph to one clear point.

| Topic sentence (from the slides) | Topic | Controlling idea |
|---|---|---|
| Online classes have become one of the most convenient ways of learning. | Online classes | have become one of the most convenient ways of learning |
| The FIFA World Cup is one of the most exciting sporting events in the world. | The FIFA World Cup | is one of the most exciting sporting events in the world |

A good topic sentence is **clear and simple**, and it **can be explained, supported or proven**. It should make the reader ask a question such as *what, where, when, why* or *how*. "Online classes are convenient" makes the reader ask *why?*, and the supporting sentences answer.

### Weak and strong topic sentences

| Weak | Problem | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| I will write about my village. | Announces the topic; no controlling idea | My village is a peaceful place to spend the holidays. |
| Dhaka has a population of over 20 million. | A fact; nothing to develop | Living in Dhaka teaches young people patience and flexibility. |
| Sports are good. | Too vague | Playing a team sport helps students build discipline and friendship. |
| Technology has changed the world in every possible way. | Too broad for one paragraph | Mobile banking has made daily payments easier for small shopkeepers. |

## Part 2: supporting sentences

Supporting sentences give **explanations, evidence and opinions** that help the reader understand the topic sentence. The slides stress *relevance*: check every sentence and ask whether it strengthens the point of the paragraph. If it does not, remove it.

The slides build support in two layers. Each **supporting detail** is followed by an **explanation or illustration**:

| Supporting detail (slides: online classes) | Explanation / illustration |
|---|---|
| Students can attend classes from any location. | For example, a student who lives in a village can join the same class as a student living in Dhaka without travelling to the university. |
| They save time and money by avoiding daily travel. | Students do not need to spend money on bus fares or fuel, and they can use the saved time for studying. |
| Recorded lectures allow students to review lessons whenever needed. | For instance, if a student misses an important grammar rule during class, they can watch the recording again before an exam. |

> **The pattern to remember:** Point → Explanation → Example. Three points, each with one or two sentences of development, give you a 150 to 200 word paragraph.

## Part 3: the concluding sentence

The concluding sentence comes last. It may **restate the main idea in different words** or **give a short summary** of the paragraph. It begins with a conclusion signal and never introduces a new idea.

| Topic sentence | Concluding sentence (slides) |
|---|---|
| Online classes have become one of the most convenient ways of learning. | In conclusion, online classes have made education more accessible and flexible for many learners. |
| The FIFA World Cup is one of the most exciting sporting events in the world. | Therefore, the FIFA World Cup continues to inspire and entertain football fans across the globe. |

## Transitional expressions

Transitions connect sentences and show the reader how ideas relate. These are the groups in the slides.

| Purpose | Expressions |
|---|---|
| Sequence / order | first, second, third, next, then, finally |
| Additional support | additionally, also, besides, equally important, furthermore, in addition, moreover |
| Example | for example, for instance, namely, specifically, to illustrate |
| Cause and effect | accordingly, consequently, hence, so, therefore, thus |
| Contrast / exception | but, however, in contrast, nevertheless, nonetheless, on the contrary, on the one hand … on the other hand, still, yet |
| Similarity | also, in the same way, likewise, similarly |
| Time | after, afterward, before, currently, during, earlier, immediately, later, meanwhile, now, recently, subsequently |
| Place / position | above, adjacent, below, beyond, here, in front, nearby, there |
| Emphasis | even, indeed, in fact, of course, truly |
| Conclusion / summary | finally, in brief, in conclusion, on the whole, thus, to conclude, to summarize, in sum, to sum up, in summary |

## Pre-writing techniques

Before writing, generate and organise your ideas. The slides list four techniques:

- **Free writing:** write non-stop for two minutes about the topic without worrying about grammar. Then pick the best ideas.
- **Scratch outline:** list the topic sentence and three supporting points in a few words. *This is what the exam asks you to write.*
- **Clustering:** write the topic in a circle and draw branches to related ideas.
- **Questioning:** ask *who, what, when, where, why* and *how* about the topic.

## The exam outline

The midterm asks you to show your outline in this exact form before the paragraph:

```text
Topic:
Topic sentence:
Supporting point 1:
Supporting point 2:
Supporting point 3:
Concluding sentence:
```

The outline is worth 1 mark and the paragraph 5. Write the topic sentence and concluding sentence in full; supporting points can be short phrases.

## Step-by-step writing method

1. **Read all the topic choices** and pick the one you can support with three clear points (1 minute).
2. **Pre-write:** jot down five or six ideas using questioning or clustering (2 minutes).
3. **Choose the best three** and put them in a logical order: time order, space order or order of importance.
4. **Write the outline** in the exam format (3 minutes).
5. **Write the paragraph:** topic sentence, then each point with its explanation and example, linked with transitions, then the concluding sentence (12 to 15 minutes).
6. **Count roughly:** 150 to 200 words is about 12 to 16 lines of handwriting.
7. **Edit** for the error types from the editing lesson (2 minutes).

## Common mistakes

- Starting with "In this paragraph I will…" instead of a topic sentence.
- A topic sentence with no controlling idea, or one too broad for 200 words.
- Points without explanation or examples ("Firstly, it is useful. Secondly, it is cheap.").
- An irrelevant sentence that drifts away from the controlling idea.
- A concluding sentence that adds a new idea.
- Splitting the answer into several paragraphs. The task asks for **one** paragraph.

## Revision summary

> **Topic sentence** (topic + controlling idea) → **Point 1** + explanation + example → **Point 2** + explanation + example → **Point 3** + explanation + example → **Concluding sentence** (restate in new words).
>
> Use transitions: *Firstly / Moreover / Finally / In conclusion*. Keep every sentence relevant. One paragraph, 150 to 200 words.

i. Topic: *Learning a second language*. Controlling idea: *opens many career opportunities*.

:::practice Practice 2 · Error identification | Find the irrelevant sentence
**Topic sentence:** Cycling is an excellent way for students to travel to campus.

1. It costs nothing once you own a bicycle.
2. It provides daily exercise without needing a gym.
3. My cousin bought a red motorbike last year.
4. Cyclists can avoid long traffic jams on narrow roads.

Which sentence does not support the topic sentence?
--- skill
Judging relevance, as the slides require
--- answer
**Sentence 3.** It is about a motorbike and a cousin, not about the advantages of cycling for students.

:::practice Practice 4 · Exam level | Outline and paragraph
Write a paragraph of 150 to 200 words on **"The benefits of joining a university club"**. Show your outline first, in the exam format.
--- skill
The complete midterm task: outline plus a three-part paragraph
--- hint
Possible points: new skills (leadership, teamwork), new friends and networks, a break from academic stress. Give each point an example.
--- answer
**Topic:** The benefits of joining a university club
**Topic sentence:** Joining a university club offers students benefits that go far beyond the classroom.
**Supporting point 1:** Develops practical skills such as leadership and teamwork
**Supporting point 2:** Builds friendships and useful connections
**Supporting point 3:** Reduces academic stress
**Concluding sentence:** In conclusion, a university club helps students grow into confident, well-connected and balanced individuals.

Joining a university club offers students benefits that go far beyond the classroom. Firstly, clubs develop practical skills that lectures alone cannot teach. For example, a member who helps organise a programming contest learns how to plan a budget, divide tasks and lead a team under pressure. Moreover, clubs help students build friendships and useful connections. Members meet seniors from different departments, and these seniors often share study tips, internship news and career advice. For instance, many students first hear about job openings through alumni who visit their club. Finally, club activities reduce academic stress. After a week of classes and assignments, an hour of debating, photography or volunteering gives students a healthy break, so they return to their studies with fresh energy. As a result, active club members often manage their time better than students who do nothing but study. In conclusion, a university club helps students grow into confident, well-connected and balanced individuals.

*(170 words.)*
:::
