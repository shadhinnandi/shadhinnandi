# English II: General Introduction

English II (ENG 1013) teaches the kind of English a university student needs every week: reading a passage and understanding what it really says, explaining an idea in a well-built paragraph, arguing a point in an essay, and writing a formal application or email that gets a clear answer. It is a practical course. Almost every mark comes from something you write.

## Why structured writing matters

At university, people judge your ideas through your writing. A teacher reading a lab report, an examiner reading an answer script and an office reading your application all ask the same questions: *What is the main point? Is it supported? Is it easy to follow?* A good structure answers those questions before the reader has to ask them.

Structure also makes writing easier for you. When you know that a paragraph needs a topic sentence, three supporting points and a concluding sentence, you no longer face an empty page. You fill a plan. This course gives you that plan for every task in the exams.

## What you will learn

| Skill | What you will be able to do |
|---|---|
| **Reading** | Skim for the main idea, scan for details, make inferences, match words to meanings and answer in your own words |
| **Summary writing** | Reduce a long passage to its key points in a short paragraph, without copying |
| **Paragraph writing** | Write a topic sentence with a clear controlling idea, support it, and close it; write descriptive, narrative, process, classification and cause-effect paragraphs |
| **Editing** | Find and correct fragments, run-ons, tense and verb-form errors, agreement errors, comma, apostrophe, article and other common mistakes |
| **Essay writing** | Plan an outline, write a thesis statement, and develop descriptive, cause-effect, argumentative and compare-contrast essays |
| **Thesis statements** | Tell a clear thesis from an announcement, a too-broad or a too-narrow statement, and complete a thesis with a parallel point |
| **Formal writing** | Write applications and emails in the format the course uses |
| **Vocabulary** | Use exam words correctly in your own sentences |

Listening and speaking are also assessed (10 marks in each exam), but they are tested live in class, so this course focuses on the reading and writing parts of the papers.

## How the exams are built

Both exams have a reading part and a writing part. Knowing the shape of each paper tells you exactly what to practise.

| | Midterm (30 marks, 1 hour 30 minutes) | Final (40 marks, 2 hours) |
|---|---|---|
| **Reading** | 12 marks: multiple choice, word meanings, short answers, summary | 9 marks: multiple choice, true/false/not given, making sentences, short answers |
| **Writing** | 8 marks: editing a paragraph (4 errors), one paragraph with its outline | 21 marks: thesis statements, editing (8 errors), an application or an email, one essay with its outline |
| **Listening and speaking** | 10 marks | 10 marks |

## Your learning roadmap

1. **Midterm preparation.** Start with reading comprehension and summary writing, then learn the structure of a paragraph and the five paragraph types, and finish with editing.
2. **Final preparation.** Build the paragraph into an essay: outlines, thesis statements and the four essay types from the slides. Then learn applications, emails, the final editing task and the vocabulary list.
3. **Question papers.** When you have finished a section, try the real Spring 2026 paper under exam time, then reveal the full solutions and compare.

## How to study with this course

> **Learn the pattern, not the answer.** Exam topics change every trimester, but the structure the examiner expects does not. If you can build an outline for *any* topic in three minutes, you are ready.

- **Read the lesson, then the models.** Each lesson explains the skill first. The model answers show the same structure applied to different topics.
- **Write before you reveal.** Every lesson ends with practice in the exam's format. Write your answer on paper, then open the hint or the model answer.
- **Do not memorise model answers.** They were written for this course to show the structure. Examiners notice memorised text, and a memorised answer rarely fits a new question.
- **Use the original slides.** Each lesson links the instructor's slides at the bottom. When a lesson and your class notes differ, follow your teacher.

Practice questions in the lessons are new questions written in the exam's style. The real past questions appear only on the two question paper pages, so you can use them as a genuine test.
