## 1. Introduction

### Define biocenosis, biosphere and ecosystem. What are the components of an ecosystem?
**Suggestion:** Ecology (components)
**Source:** Supplied solution (answer 1); the list of components is standard textbook content (the slides only define the terms)

- **Biocenosis:** a community of different organisms living together, forming a closely integrated community.
- **Biosphere:** the global ecosystem, containing living organisms (biota) and the non-living (abiotic) factors that provide them with energy and nutrients.
- **Ecosystem:** a system in which biotic (living) organisms interact with the abiotic (non-living) components of their environment. It includes all living organisms and the physical environment around them.

**Components of an ecosystem**

1. **Biotic components:** producers (green plants that make food by photosynthesis), consumers (herbivores and carnivores) and decomposers (bacteria and fungi that break down dead matter).
2. **Abiotic components:** sunlight, water, air, soil, temperature and minerals.

### What is evolution, and how is it connected to ecosystems?
**Suggestion:** Evolution
**Source:** Prepared from the slides (Lecture 1–2, slides 19–21)

**Evolution** is the process by which different kinds of living organisms developed from earlier forms during the history of the earth.

- Life begets life: every organism begins as a single cell, and its genes (in the DNA of its chromosomes) are passed from parents to offspring.
- Because offspring vary, organisms better suited to their **ecosystem** survive and reproduce more. Over many generations the ecosystem shapes which forms of life exist, and the organisms in turn change the ecosystem.
- An ecosystem is the biological community of living beings interacting with the physical environment, so ecosystems are the stage on which evolution happens.

**Source:** Supplied solution (answer 2, part 1), extended from the slides

## 2. Cell Structure

### State the principles of the modern cell theory.
**Suggestion:** Cell Theory
**Source:** Supplied solution (answer 6), checked against Lecture 3, slide 5

The **cell theory** states that the cell is the basic unit of life and that all living organisms are made of cells. The modern cell theory says:

1. All living things are made up of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living organisms.
3. New cells come only from pre-existing cells, by cell division.
4. Energy flow and metabolism occur within cells.
5. Cells contain hereditary (genetic) material, DNA, which is passed from cell to cell during division.
6. All cells are basically the same in chemical composition in organisms of similar species.

For "three fundamental principles", give points 1, 2 and 3.

### Compare active and passive transport.
**Suggestion:** Active and Passive transport
**Source:** Prepared from the slides (Lecture 3, slides 27–33)

Movement across the membrane sometimes needs energy and sometimes does not, depending on the direction of the concentration gradient.

| Passive transport | Active transport |
|---|---|
| No energy (ATP) needed | Energy (ATP) needed |
| Moves from high to low concentration, down the gradient | Moves from low to high concentration, against the gradient |
| Continues until equilibrium is reached | Can build up a concentration difference |
| Types: diffusion, osmosis (water), facilitated diffusion through channel or carrier proteins | Types: protein pumps (e.g. the Na⁺/K⁺ pump), endocytosis, exocytosis |
| Example: O₂ and CO₂ diffusing through the membrane | Example: a pump moving ions into a cell that already has more of them |

**When is an active transporter needed?** When the cell must move a solute *against* its concentration gradient, or move large particles, for example to keep a high K⁺ concentration inside the cell or to take up nutrients that are scarce outside.

### Which organelle is the "packaging centre" of the cell? How does it carry out exocytosis?
**Suggestion:** Golgi Body (Exocytosis; Diagram)
**Source:** Corrected: supplied answer 3 described the Golgi body but not exocytosis
**Review:** The exocytosis steps were added from Lecture 4, slide 11

The **Golgi apparatus (Golgi body)** is the "packaging centre" or "post office" of the cell.

1. It receives proteins and other materials from the endoplasmic reticulum at its **cis face**.
2. It modifies and sorts these materials according to their destination.
3. It packages the materials into **vesicles** at its **trans face** for transport inside or outside the cell.
4. It also helps form lysosomes.

**Exocytosis (release of material from the cell):**

1. Secretory vesicles bud off from the trans face of the Golgi body.
2. They move through the cytoplasm to the plasma membrane.
3. The vesicle membrane **fuses** with the plasma membrane.
4. The contents (proteins, hormones, enzymes) are released outside the cell.

Secretion is **constitutive** (continuous, unregulated) or **regulated** (vesicles wait for a signal before fusing).

**Example:** proteins made by the rough ER are sent to the Golgi body, where they are modified, packaged and secreted.

### Why is the lysosome called the "suicide bag"? How is an autolysosome formed?
**Suggestion:** Micro-organelles (Diagram: Autolysosome formation / Suicidal bag)
**Source:** Corrected: supplied answer 4 explained the "suicide bag" but not autolysosome formation
**Review:** The formation steps were added from Lecture 4, slides 18–20

The **lysosome** is a small, single-membrane vesicle full of **hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes**, formed from the Golgi complex. It is called the "**suicide bag**" because, if it bursts, its powerful enzymes digest the cell itself.

**Functions:**

1. Breaks down waste materials and unwanted substances inside the cell.
2. Destroys damaged or old organelles.
3. Destroys foreign bodies that enter the cell.
4. Helps in cell renewal and recycling of useful materials.

**Autolysosome formation (autophagy):**

1. **Nucleation and elongation:** a membrane (the omegasome, or phagophore) forms and grows.
2. **Autophagosome formation:** the double-membrane vesicle encloses damaged organelles or proteins.
3. **Fusion:** the autophagosome fuses with a lysosome.
4. **Autolysosome:** the fused vesicle now contains digestive enzymes.
5. **Cargo degradation:** the enzymes break the contents down.
6. **Metabolite release:** the breakdown products are recycled for the cell's use.

**Example:** a lysosome can digest a damaged organelle and recycle its useful components.

### Differentiate between prophase of mitosis and prophase I of meiosis.
**Suggestion:** Prophase of Mitosis Vs Prophase I of Meiosis
**Source:** Supplied solution (answers 9 and 10)

| Prophase I (meiosis) | Prophase (mitosis) |
|---|---|
| Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) | Homologous chromosomes do not pair |
| Crossing over occurs between non-sister chromatids | No crossing over |
| Forms tetrads, each with 4 chromatids | Tetrads are not formed |
| Chiasmata form where crossing over occurs | No chiasmata |

4. **Chiasmata:** the points where crossing over occurred become visible.
5. **Tetrads form:** each homologous pair has four chromatids.
6. The nuclear membrane breaks down and spindle fibres attach to the chromosomes.

### Draw the cell cycle, explain its stages and mark the checkpoints.
**Suggestion:** Cell Cycle (Diagram, Stages)
**Source:** Corrected: supplied answer 12 listed the checkpoints but not the stages or the diagram
**Review:** The stages were added from Cell Division, slides 13–19

The **cell cycle** is a repeating series of events: growth, DNA synthesis and cell division.

**1. Interphase** (about 90% of the cycle):

- **G1 (first gap):** the cell grows, becomes biochemically active, makes proteins and collects nucleotides and energy for DNA replication.
- **S (synthesis):** DNA replicates; each chromosome becomes two identical sister chromatids joined at the centromere. The DNA amount doubles but the chromosome number stays the same.
- **G2 (second gap):** the cell grows further, duplicates organelles, stores energy and checks the DNA for errors.

**2. Mitotic (M) phase:**

- **Prophase:** chromatin condenses into chromosomes, the nuclear envelope breaks down and spindle fibres attach to the centromeres.
- **Metaphase:** chromosomes line up at the metaphase plate.
- **Anaphase:** sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles.
- **Telophase:** nuclear envelopes form around the two sets of chromosomes.
- **Cytokinesis:** the cytoplasm divides, giving two daughter cells.

**Checkpoints** (checkpoints check the cell before it moves to the next stage and stop division if something is wrong):

1. **G1 checkpoint (restriction point):** checks cell size, nutrients, growth factors and DNA damage. If conditions are poor, the cell enters G0 (resting stage).
2. **G2 checkpoint (G2/M):** checks that DNA replication is complete and the DNA is undamaged.
3. **Metaphase (M) checkpoint:** checks that the chromosomes are correctly attached to the spindle fibres before anaphase.
4. If conditions are not correct, cell division stops until the problem is fixed. If a checkpoint fails, damaged cells keep dividing, which can lead to cancer.

::fig division-checkpoints | The three checkpoints: G1, G2 and M | Cell Division, slide 27

### What are the external and internal regulators of the cell cycle?
**Suggestion:** Cell Cycle regulators (External and Internal Regulators)
**Source:** Supplied solution (answer 13 for external signals, answer 14 for cancer genes), internal regulators added from the slides
**Review:** Answer 13 had only external signals; internal regulators were added from Lecture 4, slide 39 and Cell Division, slide 26

**External regulators** respond to events outside the cell. External signals can increase or decrease cell division depending on the needs of the cell.

- **Positive regulation:**
  1. **Growth hormone** (and growth factors): stimulates cell division. Lack of human growth hormone causes dwarfism; excess causes gigantism.
  2. **Cell growth:** large cells divide to work more efficiently, because their surface-to-volume ratio falls as they grow.
  3. **Enough nutrients:** provide the materials needed for cell division.
- **Negative regulation:**
  1. **Cell crowding:** high cell density stops further division (contact inhibition).

**Internal regulators** respond to events inside the cell and make sure a cell does not enter mitosis until all its chromosomes are replicated.

- **Cyclins:** proteins that regulate the cell cycle; injected into a non-dividing cell, cyclin causes a mitotic spindle to form.
- **Checkpoints** at G1, G2/M and metaphase halt the cycle until conditions are right (see the previous answer).

**Genes that control division (answer 14):**

- **Proto-oncogenes** normally promote cell growth and division. After a mutation they become **oncogenes** and cause uncontrolled cell division. Example: *KRAS* mutation.
- **Tumour suppressor genes** normally stop cell division and help repair damage. After a mutation, cells cannot stop dividing properly, causing cancer. Example: *TP53* mutation (found in more than 50% of human cancers).

### How does meiosis create genetic variation? Explain with diagrams.
**Suggestion:** Genetic Variation (Diagram: Crossing Over, Random Orientation)
**Source:** Supplied solution (answer 20), extended from Cell Division, slides 46 and 53–55

Two main factors create genetic variation in meiosis:

1. **Crossing over** (prophase I): homologous chromosomes exchange DNA between non-sister chromatids. The crossover points are chosen more or less at random, so each meiosis produces different **recombinant chromosomes** carrying some DNA from each parent.
2. **Random orientation** (metaphase I): homologous pairs line up at the equator in random orientations. With n chromosome pairs there are 2ⁿ possible alignments; humans (n = 23) can make 2²³, more than **eight million**, different gametes, not counting crossing over.

**Result:** the gametes are genetically different from the parent cell and from each other, which is why siblings differ and why we are not identical to our parents. Random fertilisation of one egg by one of many sperm adds even more variation.

## 4. Genetics and Genomics

### What is the central dogma? How does genetic information pass from DNA to protein?
**Suggestion:** Central Dogma / How genetic information passes from DNA to protein
**Source:** No slide in the materials (standard textbook answer)

The **central dogma of molecular biology** states that genetic information flows **DNA → RNA → protein**.

1. **Replication (DNA → DNA):** DNA copies itself before cell division.
2. **Transcription (DNA → mRNA):** in the nucleus, RNA polymerase reads one DNA strand and makes a complementary messenger RNA (U replaces T).
3. **Translation (mRNA → protein):** in the cytoplasm, ribosomes read the mRNA three bases (one **codon**) at a time; transfer RNAs (tRNA) bring the matching amino acids, which are joined by peptide bonds into a polypeptide.
4. The polypeptide folds into a working protein (enzyme, hormone, structural protein and so on).

**Flowchart:** DNA → (transcription) → mRNA → (translation at the ribosome) → polypeptide → folded protein → cell activity.

**Genetic code characteristics** (often asked with this): triplet code; universal; degenerate (several codons can code for one amino acid); non-overlapping; comma-less; has start (AUG) and stop (UAA, UAG, UGA) codons.

### Explain the major steps of DNA replication with the key enzymes.
**Suggestion:** DNA replication (Diagram)
**Source:** No slide in the materials (standard textbook answer)

DNA replication is **semi-conservative**: each new double helix keeps one old strand and gains one new strand.

1. **Initiation:** replication starts at the origin of replication. **Helicase** unwinds the double helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds, forming a replication fork; **single-strand binding proteins** keep the strands apart; **topoisomerase (gyrase)** relieves the twisting ahead of the fork.
2. **Priming:** **primase** lays down a short RNA primer, because DNA polymerase can only extend an existing strand.
3. **Elongation:** **DNA polymerase III** adds complementary nucleotides in the **5' → 3'** direction. The **leading strand** is made continuously; the **lagging strand** is made in short **Okazaki fragments**, because the strands are antiparallel.
4. **Primer removal:** **DNA polymerase I** replaces the RNA primers with DNA.
5. **Joining:** **DNA ligase** seals the gaps between the Okazaki fragments.
6. **Proofreading:** DNA polymerase checks and corrects mismatched bases.

**Diagram to draw:** a replication fork with helicase at the fork, the leading strand (continuous arrow) and the lagging strand (Okazaki fragments with primers, polymerase and ligase).

::fig genetics-antiparallel-strands | The antiparallel strands are the reason for leading and lagging strands | Lecture 7, slide 10

### Compare DNA and RNA. Which is better as genetic material, and why?
**Suggestion:** DNA Vs RNA
**Source:** Prepared from the slides (Lecture 7, slides 3–10), with standard stability points

| Feature | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar | Deoxyribose (no –OH at 2') | Ribose (–OH at 2') |
| Bases | A, G, C, **T** | A, G, C, **U** |
| Strands | Double-stranded helix | Usually single-stranded |
| Stability | Very stable | Less stable, easily broken down |
| Location | Mostly nucleus (also mitochondria, chloroplasts) | Nucleus and cytoplasm |
| Function | Stores genetic information long term | Carries the message and makes proteins (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA) |

**DNA is the better genetic (storage) material:**

1. **Chemical stability:** deoxyribose lacks the 2'-OH group, so DNA is far less reactive and less easily broken than RNA.
2. **Double strand:** each strand is a template for the other, so damage on one strand can be repaired from the complementary strand.
3. **Thymine instead of uracil:** cytosine can turn into uracil by damage; with T as the normal base, the cell can recognise and repair such errors.
4. **Accurate replication** with proofreading.

RNA is better suited to short-lived jobs such as carrying messages. (Some viruses use RNA as their genome, which is why they mutate quickly.)

### State Mendel's principles of inheritance with examples.
**Suggestion:** Mendel's principles of inheritance
**Source:** No slide in the materials (standard textbook answer)

1. **Law of dominance:** when two different alleles are present, one (dominant) is expressed and the other (recessive) is hidden. *Example:* crossing tall (TT) with dwarf (tt) pea plants gives all tall (Tt) plants in F1.
2. **Law of segregation:** the two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete carries only one allele. *Example:* selfing Tt gives TT, Tt and tt in a **3 : 1** tall-to-dwarf ratio in F2.
3. **Law of independent assortment:** alleles of different genes are inherited independently of each other. *Example:* a dihybrid cross of round-yellow (RRYY) and wrinkled-green (rryy) peas gives a **9 : 3 : 3 : 1** ratio in F2.

### What are the types of chromosomal abnormalities? How does trisomy cause Down syndrome?
**Suggestion:** Types of chromosomal abnormalities
**Source:** Supplied solution (answer 19, non-disjunction), extended

**A. Numerical abnormalities** (wrong number of chromosomes, aneuploidy):

- **Trisomy** (2n + 1): one extra chromosome. Down syndrome is trisomy 21; also Edwards (18) and Patau (13).
- **Monosomy** (2n − 1): one missing chromosome, e.g. Turner syndrome (45, X).
- **Polyploidy:** whole extra sets of chromosomes (3n, 4n), common in plants.

**B. Structural abnormalities** (part of a chromosome changed): **deletion** (a segment lost, e.g. cri-du-chat, 5p deletion), **duplication**, **inversion** (a segment reversed) and **translocation** (a segment moved to another chromosome).

**Non-disjunction (answer 19):** chromosomes do not separate properly during meiosis.

1. Chromosomes fail to separate.
2. Some gametes get an extra or a missing chromosome.
3. Fertilisation then gives a zygote with an abnormal number, causing genetic abnormalities.
4. Example: **Down syndrome**, three copies of chromosome 21. Its risk rises with the mother's age.

**Detection:** a **karyotype** (chromosomes photographed at metaphase and arranged in pairs by size) shows extra, missing or broken chromosomes.

### How can Cryo-EM be used to determine the 3D structure of biological macromolecules?
**Suggestion:** 2 experimental methods to determine protein structures (Cryo-EM)
**Source:** Supplied solution (answer 16); no slide in the materials

**Cryo-electron microscopy (Cryo-EM)** is a technique used to find the 3D structure of biomolecules by freezing the sample and taking many electron images.

**Process:**

1. Freeze the biomolecule very quickly in a thin layer of ice, so it stays in its natural shape.
2. Send electrons through the frozen sample.
3. Take many 2D pictures of molecules lying in different orientations.
4. A computer combines the pictures.
5. Get the 3D structure.

**Advantage:** no crystallisation is needed, and very large complexes can be studied.

### How does NMR determine protein structure?
**Suggestion:** 2 experimental methods to determine protein structures (NMR)
**Source:** Supplied solution (answer 18); no slide in the materials

**Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy** finds the 3D structure of biomolecules by studying signals from atoms such as ¹H and ¹³C.

**Process:**

1. Put the sample in a strong magnetic field.
2. Apply radio waves to the atoms.
3. The atomic nuclei absorb and release energy.
4. This produces signals.
5. A computer analyses the signals (the distances and angles between atoms).
6. The signals give the 3D structure.

**Advantage:** biomolecules can be studied in their natural solution state, including their movements.
