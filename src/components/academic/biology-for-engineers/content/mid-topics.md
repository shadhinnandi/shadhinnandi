## 1. Introduction: biology for engineers

**The living world (Lecture 1–2, slide 18).**

- **Biocenosis:** an association of different organisms forming a closely integrated community.
- **Biosphere:** the global ecosystem, made of living organisms (biota) and the non-living (abiotic) factors that give them energy and nutrients.
- **Ecosystem:** the interaction between organisms and their surrounding environment, i.e. the biotic community together with its abiotic environment.
- **Evolution:** the process by which different kinds of living organisms developed from earlier forms during the history of the earth (slide 21).

**Biology in engineering (slides 23–41).**

| Technology | What it is |
|---|---|
| Organ-on-a-chip | A microfluidic chip holding real organ cells, with body-fluid-like liquid flowing past them, to test drugs or toxins without animal studies |
| Biosensor | A device that uses a biological reaction to measure a quantity, such as an analyte concentration (glucose meters, pregnancy tests, wound-monitoring plasters) |
| Biopesticide | Organisms (e.g. *Bacillus thuringiensis*) used instead of chemicals for pest control |
| Self-healing concrete | Bacteria in the concrete form calcium carbonate under suitable conditions, filling cracks |
| Tissue engineering | Cells grown on scaffolds to repair or replace tissue, e.g. artificial skin for burns |
| Biochip | A miniature laboratory running thousands of biochemical reactions at once |

## 2. Cell structure

**Modern cell theory (Lecture 3, slide 5).** All living things are made of one or more cells; all cells arise from pre-existing cells by division; the cell is the basic unit of structure and function; energy flow (metabolism) happens inside cells; cells contain hereditary information (DNA); cells of similar species have the same basic chemical composition.

**Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells (slide 8).** Prokaryotes (bacteria, archaea) are usually unicellular, 0.1–5 µm, with circular DNA not enclosed in a nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles and division by binary fission. Eukaryotes (plants, animals, fungi, protists) are 10–100 µm, with linear DNA inside a nucleus, membrane-bound organelles (ER, lysosomes, peroxisomes), microtubules, and division by mitosis and meiosis.

**The fluid mosaic model (slide 24).** Proposed by Singer and Nicolson in 1974. The membrane is **fluid** because its lipids and proteins move sideways, letting it bend and change shape. It is a **mosaic** because many different molecules are pieced together: a phospholipid bilayer (hydrophilic heads outside, hydrophobic tails inside), cholesterol, integral and peripheral proteins, glycoproteins and glycolipids.

::fig cell-membrane-proteins | Integral, peripheral and lipid-anchored membrane proteins in the bilayer | Lecture 3, slide 19

**Transport (slide 27).**

| Passive transport | Active transport |
|---|---|
| No energy (ATP) needed | Needs energy (ATP) |
| High to low concentration (down the gradient) | Low to high concentration (against the gradient) |
| Diffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion | Protein pumps (e.g. Na⁺/K⁺ pump), endocytosis, exocytosis |

**Organelles (Lecture 4).** The **rough ER** has ribosomes and makes and transports proteins; the **smooth ER** has none and makes lipids and steroids. The **Golgi body** is the cell's "packaging centre" or "post office": it receives material from the ER, modifies and sorts it, packs it into vesicles and sends it out by **exocytosis**. It also forms lysosomes. **Lysosomes** are single-membrane sacs of hydrolytic enzymes, the "suicide bags" of the cell. **Autophagy** recycles damaged parts: an autophagosome fuses with a lysosome to form an **autolysosome**, which digests the contents (slide 20).

::fig organelles-autolysosome-formation | Autolysosome formation: omegasome, autophagosome, fusion with a lysosome, autolysosome and cargo degradation | Lecture 4, slide 20

## 3. Cell division

**The cell cycle (Cell Division, slides 13–19).** Interphase takes about 90% of the cycle: **G1** (growth, proteins and nucleotides made), **S** (DNA replication; each chromosome becomes two sister chromatids) and **G2** (more growth, organelles duplicated, DNA checked). The **mitotic phase** follows: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase (PMAT), then cytokinesis.

**Control of the cycle (slides 22–28).**

- **External positive signals:** human growth hormone, cell growth (a falling surface-to-volume ratio), enough nutrients.
- **External negative signal:** cell crowding (contact inhibition).
- **Internal checkpoints:** **G1** (restriction point: growth factors, nutrients, cell size, DNA damage; otherwise the cell enters G0), **G2/M** (DNA replication complete and undamaged) and **metaphase–anaphase** (chromosomes correctly attached to the spindle).

::fig division-checkpoints | The G1, G2 and M checkpoints of the cell cycle | Cell Division, slide 27

**Cancer (slides 30–33).** **Proto-oncogenes** stimulate division; mutated, they become **oncogenes** (e.g. *KRAS*). **Tumour suppressor genes** stop division and repair damage; mutated, they let cells divide out of control (e.g. *TP53*, mutated in more than 50% of human cancers).

**Meiosis (slides 34–55).** One diploid (2n) cell gives **four genetically different haploid (n) cells**. In **prophase I**, homologous chromosomes pair (**synapsis**), non-sister chromatids exchange segments (**crossing over**) at **chiasmata**, and each pair forms a **tetrad** of four chromatids. **Genetic variation** comes from crossing over and the **random orientation** of homologous pairs in metaphase I (2²³, over eight million combinations in humans).

::fig division-prophase-i-tetrad | Synapsis, crossing over and tetrad formation in prophase I | Cell Division, slide 41

## 4. Genetics and genomics

**Nucleotides (Lecture 7, slides 3–6).** A nucleotide has three parts: a **phosphate group**, a **pentose sugar** (ribose in RNA, deoxyribose in DNA) and a **nitrogenous base**. Purines (double ring) are adenine and guanine; pyrimidines (single ring) are cytosine, thymine (DNA only) and uracil (RNA only).

**DNA (slides 7–10).** A double helix (Watson and Crick) with a sugar-phosphate backbone outside and bases inside. A pairs with T and G with C by hydrogen bonds, so the strands are **complementary**; they run in opposite directions, 5'→3' and 3'→5', so they are **antiparallel**.

**Gene (slide 13).** A specific DNA sequence at a locus on a chromosome that codes for a protein or a functional RNA. Humans have about 20,000–25,000 genes; different forms of a gene are alleles.

::fig genetics-dna-structure | The structure of DNA: double helix, base pairing and antiparallel strands | Lecture 7, slide 7

## 5. Structural biology

**Biomolecules and their building blocks:** proteins (amino acids), carbohydrates (monosaccharides such as glucose), lipids (fatty acids and glycerol) and nucleic acids (nucleotides).

**Protein structure:** **primary** (the amino acid sequence), **secondary** (α-helices and β-sheets held by hydrogen bonds), **tertiary** (the 3D fold of one chain) and **quaternary** (several chains together).

::fig db-protein-structure-levels | The four levels of protein structure | Biological Databases lecture, slide 20

**Methods for 3D structure:** **Cryo-EM** freezes the sample and combines thousands of 2D electron micrographs into a 3D model; **NMR** places the sample in a strong magnetic field and reads radio-frequency signals from atomic nuclei (¹H, ¹³C) to calculate the structure in solution. X-ray crystallography is the third major method.
